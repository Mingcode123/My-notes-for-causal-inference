Lecture 7: Balancing Estimators

Balance guides the construction of \(\widehat e(x)\).

Balance / the design principle for \(\widehat e(x)\) \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) Construct a balancing loss for propensity estimation; high-dimensional ATE estimation.

Two aims are constructing a balancing loss for propensity estimation, and estimating ATEs with high-dimensional covariates. IPW and AIPW use propensity scores; this lecture uses convex optimization to derive how to estimate \(e(x)\).

Review of IPW

For IID observations \((X_i,Y_i,W_i)\in\mathcal X\times\mathbb R\times\{0,1\}\), the goal is \(\tau=\mu_{(1)}-\mu_{(0)}\), where \(\mu_{(w)}=E[Y_i(w)]\). Assume \[\{Y_i(0),Y_i(1)\}\perp W_i\mid X_i,\qquad 0<\eta\leq e(X_i)\leq1-\eta<1,\] with \(e(x)=P(W_i=1\mid X_i=x)\) and positive constant \(\eta\). Estimate the treated mean using \[\widehat\mu_{\rm IPW}(1)=\frac1n\sum_i\frac{W_iY_i}{\widehat e(X_i)}. \tag{7.3}\] A brief consistency argument has three steps:

  1. Population balance: under unconfoundedness, \(\mu_{(1)}=E[W_iY_i/e(X_i)]\).

  2. Oracle estimator: with the true \(e(x)\) it is unbiased; overlap and \(E[Y(1)^2]<\infty\) give finite variance.

  3. Feasible approximation: if the estimated propensities also satisfy overlap, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives \[\begin{aligned} |\widehat\mu_{\rm IPW}(1)-\widehat\mu^*_{\rm IPW}(1)| &\leq\left[\frac1n\sum_i\left(\frac1{\widehat e(X_i)}-\frac1{e(X_i)}\right)^2\right]^{1/2} \left[\frac1n\sum_i(W_iY_i)^2\right]^{1/2}\\ &\leq\frac1{\eta^2}\left[\frac1n\sum_i(\widehat e(X_i)-e(X_i))^2\right]^{1/2} \left[\frac1n\sum_i(W_iY_i)^2\right]^{1/2}. \end{aligned}\] Recall \(|a\cdot b|^2\leq\|a\|^2\|b\|^2\). Under the product-rate condition, the analogous AIPW error is negligible relative to \(1/\sqrt n\) as \(n\to\infty\).

Population versus sample balance

Expand the conditional mean in a predefined basis: \[\mu_{(w)}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^\infty\beta_{j(w)}\psi_j(x).\]

Polynomial or Fourier basis \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) \(\psi_j(x)\) in the conditional-mean expansion.

Under reasonable regularity conditions, \[\mu_{(w)}=\sum_{j=1}^\infty\beta_{j(w)}E[\psi_j(X_i)].\]

Take expectations on both sides \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) \(\mu_{(w)}=\sum_j\beta_{j(w)}E[\psi_j(X)]\).

Could these regularity assumptions have a problem?

Supplementary explanation.

A sufficient condition for exchanging sum and expectation is \(\sum_j|\beta_{j(w)}|E|\psi_j(X)|<\infty\). Convergence of partial sums in \(L^1\) also suffices; \(L^2\) convergence under a probability distribution implies it. Pointwise convergence alone does not. Population balance can also be proved directly by conditioning on \(X\).

With unconfoundedness, \(Y_i=\mu_{(W_i)}(X_i)+\varepsilon_i\) and \(E[\varepsilon_i\mid X_i,W_i]=0\). The population IPW argument is \[\begin{aligned} E\left[\frac{W_iY_i}{e(X_i)}\right] &=E\left[\frac{W_i}{e(X_i)}\sum_j\beta_{j(1)}\psi_j(X_i)\right]\\ &=\sum_j\beta_{j(1)}E\left[\frac{W_i\psi_j(X_i)}{e(X_i)}\right] =\sum_j\beta_{j(1)}E[\psi_j(X_i)]=\mu_{(1)}. \end{aligned}\]

The core: \(E[W_i\mid X_i]=e(X_i)\) \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) \(E[W_i\psi_j(X_i)/e(X_i)]=E[\psi_j(X_i)]\).

Thus choose weights so that, in the sample, \[\frac1n\sum_i\frac{W_i\psi_j(X_i)}{\widehat e(X_i)} \approx\frac1n\sum_i\psi_j(X_i),\qquad j=1,2,\ldots. \tag{7.8}\]

A balancing loss for propensity estimation

Suppose the outcome model is linear, \(\mu_{(w)}(x)=x\beta_{(w)}\), and the propensity model is logistic, \[e(x)=\frac1{1+\exp(-x\theta)}.\] sample balance then involves only the covariates \(X_i\), and the targeted balance concerns means. Equation (7.8) becomes \[\frac1n\sum_i(1+e^{-X_i\widehat\theta})W_iX_i=\frac1n\sum_iX_i. \tag{7.9}\] This is the KKT condition of the convex problem \[\widehat\theta=\arg\min_\theta\frac1n\sum_i\ell_\theta(X_i,Y_i,W_i),\qquad \ell_\theta(X_i,Y_i,W_i)=W_ie^{-X_i\theta}+(1-W_i)X_i\theta. \tag{7.10}\] It can therefore be solved by Newton descent.

Balance equation (7.9) is the convex loss KKT condition \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) Newton descent when the loss has a suitable finite minimizer.

This serves as the balancing loss function. The Hessian \(n^{-1}\sum_iW_ie^{-X_i\theta}X_i^\mathsf TX_i\) is positive semidefinite. A unique finite minimizer needs sufficient rank and existence; exact balance may fail if the target mean lies outside the treated covariate convex support. For arm-specific balance, one may fit two calibration losses, one per arm. The true treatment mechanism still has a single propensity \(e(x)\) and control probability \(1-e(x)\).

ATE estimation with high-dimensional confounders

Suppose \(X_i\in\mathbb R^p\), possibly \(p\gg n\), and \(\mu_{(w)}(x)=x\beta_{(w)}\). In high dimensions, it is difficult to estimate \(e(x)\) adequately; exact weights are not attainable, so seek approximate balance. By Holder’s inequality, \[\begin{aligned} &\frac1n\sum_i\frac{W_i\mu_{(1)}(X_i)}{\widehat e(X_i)}-\frac1n\sum_i\mu_{(1)}(X_i)\\ &\quad=\left(\frac1n\sum_i\frac{W_iX_i}{\widehat e(X_i)}-\frac1n\sum_iX_i\right)\beta_{(1)}\\ &\quad\leq\left\|\frac1n\sum_i\frac{W_iX_i}{\widehat e(X_i)}-\frac1n\sum_iX_i\right\|_\infty\|\beta_{(1)}\|_1. \end{aligned}\] The first norm measures imbalance. Holder’s bound is \(\sum_i|a_ib_i|\leq(\sum_i|a_i|^p)^{1/p}(\sum_i|b_i|^q)^{1/q}\).

Personal interpretation and questions.

Good inverse-probability weights make the worst case of imbalance as good as possible. Two problems remain: (1) how to parameterize \(\widehat e(X_i)\) to obtain sup-norm approximate balance; (2) the bound is meaningful only if \(\|\beta_{(1)}\|_1\) is bounded. High-dimensional statistics usually assumes a sparse coefficient vector rather than constraining its \(1\)-norm directly.

Supplement: norm reminders.

The zero-count is \(\|x\|_0=\sum_i\mathbf1\{x_i\ne0\}\); it is not a norm. The \(1\)-norm is \(\sum_i|x_i|\), the \(2\)-norm is \((\sum_ix_i^2)^{1/2}\), and the infinity norm is \(\max_i|x_i|\). For \(p\ge1\), \(\|x\|_p=(\sum_i|x_i|^p)^{1/p}\). Holders inequality requires \(p,q\ge1\) and \(1/p+1/q=1\), including \((1,\infty)\). This endpoint bounds the absolute imbalance error above.

Approximate balancing weights and regression augmentation

For a well-chosen tuning parameter \(\zeta\), solve \[\widehat\gamma=\arg\min_{\gamma_i\geq1} \left\{\frac1{n^2}\|\gamma\|_2^2+ \zeta\left\|\frac1n\sum_i(\gamma_iW_i-1)X_i\right\|_\infty^2\right\}. \tag{7.12}\]

Squared \(L_2\) size; imbalance; tuning weight \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) \(n^{-2}\|\gamma\|_2^2\); the squared infinity norm; \(\zeta\).

Then use \(1/\widehat e(X_i)=\widehat\gamma_i\) as inverse-propensity weights. Sub-Gaussian concentration inequalities verify, for oracle weights, bounds of the form \[\frac1{n^2}\|1/e(X_i)\|_2^2\leq\frac{\eta^{-2}}n, \qquad \left\|\frac1n\sum_i(\gamma_iW_i-1)X_i\right\|_\infty^2 =O_p\left(\frac{\eta^{-2}\log p}{n}\right).\] \(\widehat e(X_i)\) need not estimate \(e(X_i)\) well; with a suitable \(\zeta\), these inverse weights nonetheless satisfy a bias bound. Next augment the weighted estimator with high-dimensional regression to remove dependence on \(\|\beta_{(1)}\|_1\). The augmented balancing estimator is \[\widehat\mu_{\rm AB}(1)=\frac1n\sum_i \left[\widehat\mu_{(1)}(X_i)+\widehat\gamma_iW_i(Y_i-\widehat\mu_{(1)}(X_i))\right], \quad\widehat\mu_{(1)}(X_i)=X_i\widehat\beta_{(1)}. \tag{7.14}\] Its decomposition is \[\begin{aligned} \widehat\mu_{\rm AB}(1)={}&\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_iX_i\beta_{(1)}}_{\text{\textcolor{red}{mean of }}\textcolor{red}{\mu_{(1)}(X_i)}} +\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_i\widehat\gamma_iW_i(Y_i-X_i\beta_{(1)})}_{\text{\textcolor{red}{mean-zero noise}}}\\ &+\left[\frac1n\sum_i(1-\widehat\gamma_iW_i)X_i\right](\widehat\beta_{(1)}-\beta_{(1)}). \end{aligned}\] The last term is bounded in magnitude by

\[\left\|\frac1n\sum_i(1-\widehat\gamma_iW_i)X_i\right\|_\infty \|\widehat\beta_{(1)}-\beta_{(1)}\|_1.\]

Weights need only correct \(\beta_{(1)}-\widehat\beta_{(1)}\), not the whole coefficient vector \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The imbalance times regression-error remainder.

High-dimensional regression supplies a bound on this error: under sparsity \(\|\beta_{(1)}\|_0\leq k\) and a restricted-eigenvalue condition, lasso achieves \[\|\widehat\beta_{(1)}-\beta_{(1)}\|_1 =O_p\left(k\sqrt{\frac{\log p}{n}}\right).\]

Putting the steps together.

Suppose the design satisfies the conditions for this bound, \(\beta_{(1)}\) is \(k\)-sparse, and \(k\ll\sqrt n/\log p\).

  1. Fit lasso on the treated units; the coefficient \(1\)-norm error is \(o_p(1/\sqrt{\log p})\).

  2. Fit \(\widehat\gamma\) using (7.12), giving infinity-norm imbalance \(O_p(\sqrt{\log(p)/n})\).

  3. Use (7.14), and insert these bounds into the decomposition: \[\widehat\mu_{\rm AB}(1)-\frac1n\sum_iX_i\beta_{(1)} =\frac1n\sum_i\widehat\gamma_iW_i(Y_i-X_i\beta_{(1)})+o_p(n^{-1/2}). \tag{7.17}\] The noise term is \(O_p(n^{-1/2})\). These rates use controlled sub-Gaussian covariates, appropriate lasso tuning, restricted eigenvalues and noise moments. Weights depend on \((X,W)\) rather than outcome noise. Studentization needs no dominant weighted residual and justification for substituting estimated coefficients.

A central limit theorem also holds: \[\frac{\sum_i\widehat\gamma_iW_i(Y_i-X_i\beta_{(1)})} {\sqrt{\sum_i\widehat\gamma_i^2W_i^2(Y_i-X_i\beta_{(1)})^2}} \Rightarrow N(0,1).\]

Personal caveat.

This is weaker than expected: it only gives a confidence interval for the sample average \(\overline\mu_{(1)}=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i\beta_{(1)}\), not for \(\mu_{(1)}\) itself. To obtain a result for the population mean, one possible route is to establish convergence of the estimated weights \(\widehat\gamma_i\).

For the population target, also include \(n^{-1}\sum_i\mu_{(1)}(X_i)-E[\mu_{(1)}(X)]\) and its joint limit with the weighted noise. Convergence of the weights to a stable function is one sufficient route.
