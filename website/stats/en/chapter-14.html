Lecture 14: Adaptive experiments

Adaptive experiments dynamically adjust the experimental design. Earlier fixed experiments worked in an IID setting, an assumption that is sometimes too restrictive.

Setting and notation

There are actions \(k=1,\ldots,K\) and subjects \(t=1,\ldots,T\). Across subjects, potential outcomes are IID. Write \[Y_t(k)\sim F_k,\qquad Y_t=Y_t(W_t),\qquad \mu_k=\mathbb E[Y_t(k)].\] \(W_t\) is the action selected for subject \(t\), \(F_k\) is the potential-outcome distribution for arm \(k\), and \(\mu_k\) is its mean. Define regret by \[R_T=\sum_{t=1}^T(\mu^*-\mu_{W_t}),\qquad \mu^*=\sup_{1\le k\le K}\mu_k.\] \(\mu^*\) is the largest expected reward among the available actions. Regret is the expected reward lost by choosing a sequence of actions instead of always choosing the best action.

Define \[n_{kt}=\sum_{j=1}^t\mathbf1\{W_j=k\},\qquad \widehat\mu_{kt}=\frac1{n_{kt}}\sum_{j=1}^t\mathbf1\{W_j=k\}Y_j.\]

\(n_{kt}\) \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) Number of pulls of arm \(k\) through time \(t\).

\(\widehat\mu_{kt}\) \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) Average reward of arm \(k\).

These use the information collected from time \(1\) through \(t\).

My restatement.

Think of \(T\) similar, independent individuals. Each has \(K\) treatment options and hence \(K\) potential outcomes, with the same arm-specific distributions \(F_k\). For each individual we observe only the outcome of the chosen treatment, \(Y_t=Y_t(W_t)\). The vector \(\mu\) collects expected rewards. Total regret adds, across the \(T\) individuals, the difference between the chosen treatment and the best treatment; the best arm is the same arm throughout this setup.

In an ordinary randomized experiment, \(W_t\) is uniform and nonadaptive over \(1,\ldots,K\), so regret grows linearly with \(T\): \[R_T\sim T\sum_{k=1}^K\frac{\mu^*-\mu_k}{K}.\] The summand average is the expected loss per individual. The first objective of an adaptive experiment is sublinear regret.

Regret should not grow linearly with the number of experiments or time; choices get closer to the optimum. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) Sublinear regret.

This requires balancing exploration (trying things) with exploitation (using what is known).

Optimism in the face of uncertainty

The upper confidence bound (UCB) method balances exploration and exploitation:

  1. Initialize by sampling each arm \(t_0\) times: choose it and observe it \(t_0\) times, for \(Kt_0\) initial samples.

  2. For each \(t=Kt_0+1,Kt_0+2,\ldots\), use data collected through \(t-1\) to construct a confidence interval \(\widehat U_{kt}\) for each \(\mu_k\).

  3. Select the action \(W_t\) whose interval has the largest upper endpoint and observe \(Y_t=Y_t(W_t)\).

Each new time point adds only one observation.

To understand how UCB controls regret, simplify to Gaussian rewards: \[Y_t(k)\sim\mathcal N(\mu_k,b^2),\tag{14.4}\] with known \(b^2\).

Known variance simplifies the calculation. \(\textcolor{blue}{\longrightarrow}\) \(b^2\) in the Gaussian UCB radius.

Use \[\widehat U_{kt}=\widehat\mu_{k,t-1} \ \pm\ b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{n_{k,t-1}}}.\tag{14.5}\] For \(t_0=1\) and \(T\ge\max\{K,2\}\), the regret upper bound is \[R_T\le\sum_{k:\mu_k<\mu_{k^*}} \left\{\frac{16b^2\log T}{\mu_{k^*}-\mu_k} +(\mu_{k^*}-\mu_k)\right\},\tag{14.6}\] with probability at least \(1-K/T\), where \(k^*\) denotes the best arm.

My intuition about this bound.

The gap \(\mu_{k^*}-\mu_k\) in the denominator. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) Closer arms take longer to distinguish; far worse arms can be discarded quickly.

UCB regret grows only logarithmically. A small gap \(\mu_{k^*}-\mu_k\) appears to send the displayed bound to infinity. In practice there are two competing bounds: for a strong effect, UCB discards an arm when its interval is separated; for a weak effect, total regret is at most \(T(\mu_{k^*}-\mu_k)\). Combining these gives a finite bound, and for uniformly bounded mean gaps it gives an order \(K\sqrt{T\log T}\) upper bound.

On the confidence event, each arm contributes at most \(\min\{T\Delta_k,16b^2\log T/\Delta_k+\Delta_k\}\). The uniform \(O(K\sqrt{T\log T})\) comparison uses fixed \(b\) and uniformly bounded gaps; the initial pulls alone cost \(\sum_k\Delta_k\).

A regret bound for UCB

Write regret as \[R_T=\sum_{k:\Delta_k>0}n_{kT}\Delta_k,\qquad \Delta_k=\mu^*-\mu_k.\] The main task is to control how often UCB selects a suboptimal arm.

This is also how I understand the design of UCB. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) Control the number of pulls \(n_{kT}\).

Let \(\zeta_{kj}\) be the time of the \(j\)th pull of arm \(k\). For each arm, imagine an IID reward stream \(Y'_\ell(k)\sim\mathcal N(\mu_k,b^2)\), revealed one observation at a time when that arm is selected. Then \[\begin{aligned} &P\left[\sup_{K<t\le T}\left\{ \mu_k-\widehat\mu_{k,t-1}-b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{n_{k,t-1}}}\right\}\ge0\right]\\ &\quad\le P\left[\sup_{1\le j\le n_{kT}}\left\{ \mu_k-\widehat\mu_{k,\zeta_{kj}}-b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{j}}\right\}\ge0\right]\\ &\quad=P\left[\sup_{1\le j\le n_{kT}}\left\{ \mu_k-\frac1j\sum_{\ell=1}^jY'_\ell(k)-b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{j}}\right\}\ge0\right]\\ &\quad\le P\left[\sup_{1\le j\le T}\left\{ \mu_k-\frac1j\sum_{\ell=1}^jY'_\ell(k)-b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{j}}\right\}\ge0\right] \le\frac1T. \end{aligned}\]

Enlarging the index set can only increase the supremum and its event. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) \(K<t\le T\ \leadsto\ 1\le j\le n_{kT}\ \leadsto\ 1\le j\le T\)

The reindexing replaces calendar time by the number of observations from the same arm. The final concentration step is the part I wanted to check.

My question about Hoeffding’s inequality.

For independent random variables \(Z_1,\ldots,Z_n\) with \(Z_i\in[a,b]\) almost surely, and \(s>0\), \[P\left(\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n(Z_i-\mathbb E[Z_i])\ge s\right) \le\exp\left(-\frac{2ns^2}{(b-a)^2}\right).\] The lower tail has the same bound, and the two-sided bound has a factor \(2\).

Almost surely bounded. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) \(Z_i\in[a,b]\)

For events \(A_1,\ldots,A_n\), \[P(A_1\cup\cdots\cup A_n)\le\sum_{i=1}^nP(A_i).\] I connect this union bound with the desired confidence level \(1-K/T\).

My attempted calculation: perhaps it is enough to show \[P\left(\mu_k-\frac1j\sum_{\ell=1}^jY'_\ell(k) -b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{j}}\ge0\right)\le\frac1T.\] The left side can be written as \[P\left(\frac1j\sum_{\ell=1}^jY'_\ell(k)-\mu_k \le-b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{j}}\right).\] Trying to use the bounded-variable inequality leads me to the expression \[\exp\left(-\frac{2j\,b^2(4\log T)/j}{(b-a)^2}\right) =\exp\left(-\frac{8b^2}{(b-a)^2}\log T\right) \ \overset{?}{\le}\ \frac1T.\] But \(Y'_\ell(k)\) is unbounded. Is the \(k\) written incorrectly? I do not know how the last step is simplified; perhaps there is an unstated assumption.

For the arm-count argument, we need a simultaneous event of probability at least \(1-K/T\): \[|\widehat\mu_{k,t-1}-\mu_k| \le b\sqrt{\frac{4\log T}{n_{k,t-1}}} \quad\text{for all }k,\ t=K+1,\ldots,T.\tag{14.8}\]

Supplementary derivation: the Gaussian concentration step

The Gaussian assumption supplies the tail bound, without bounded rewards. Let \(\overline Y'_{kj}=j^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^jY'_\ell(k)\). For \(T\ge2\), \[\sqrt j(\overline Y'_{kj}-\mu_k)/b\sim N(0,1),\qquad P\left(|\overline Y'_{kj}-\mu_k|>b\sqrt{4\log T/j}\right) =2\Phi(-2\sqrt{\log T})\le T^{-2}.\] The last inequality follows from the normal tail bound \(2\Phi(-x)\le2e^{-x^2/2}/(x\sqrt{2\pi})\). A union bound over at most \(KT\) arm/count pairs establishes (14.8). A bound of only \(1/T\) for each count would not be enough before taking the union over counts. The \(b\) denoting a Gaussian standard deviation is also distinct from an endpoint in the bounded-variable inequality. The lecture’s two occurrences of \(Y'_\ell(0)\) in this derivation should read \(Y'_\ell(k)\).

Continuing the arm-count argument.

On the event (14.8), write \(r_{kt}=b\sqrt{4\log T/n_{k,t-1}}\). If \(W_t=k\), the UCB rule gives \[\begin{aligned} \mu^*&\le\widehat\mu_{k^*,t-1}+r_{k^*t}\\ &\le\widehat\mu_{k,t-1}+r_{kt}\\ &\le\mu_k+2r_{kt}. \end{aligned}\] The first and last steps use the two different sides of (14.8). Hence, for \(\Delta_k>0\), \[n_{k,t-1}\le\frac{16b^2\log T}{\Delta_k^2},\qquad n_{kT}\le\frac{16b^2\log T}{\Delta_k^2}+1.\]

Confidence event (1), followed by UCB selecting \(k\). \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) Pull-count threshold (2): \(n_{k,t-1}\le16b^2\log T/\Delta_k^2\).

My interpretation: choosing arm \(k\) means that its upper confidence endpoint must still exceed that of the best arm. Once its count passes the threshold, which grows only with \(\log T\), this argument rules out choosing it again.

The two sketches compare growth rates and illustrate crossing a threshold.

Substituting the count bound into regret gives \[R_T=\sum_{k:\Delta_k>0}\Delta_k n_{kT} \ \le\ \sum_{k:\Delta_k>0}\left\{\frac{16b^2\log T}{\Delta_k}+\Delta_k\right\}.\]

The final additive gap \(\Delta_k\). \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The regret of the initial \(t_0=1\) pull; the first term covers the remaining pulls.

Adaptive randomization schemes

UCB has limitations. It is difficult to combine with methods developed for IID sampling and to extend to complicated sampling designs. Arm-specific variances \(b_k^2\) can differ, requiring comparable confidence intervals based on different standard errors.

UCB chooses \(W_t\) using past observations. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) Many familiar methods, including statistical tests and conditional-mean model fitting, use randomized IID data.

Thompson sampling.

This is an alternative to UCB: a heuristic algorithm based on Bayesian updating.

  1. Choose a prior \(\Pi_{k,0}\) for each potential-outcome distribution \(F_k\).

  2. At time \(t\), compute the posterior probability that arm \(k\) is the best arm: \[e_{k,t-1}=P_{\Pi_{t-1}}(\mu_k=\mu^*).\]

  3. Randomly select an action: \[W_t\sim\operatorname{Categorical}(e_{\cdot,t-1}).\]

  4. Observe \(Y_t=Y_t(W_t)\) and update the posterior to \(\Pi_t\).

Thompson sampling and UCB have similar statistical behavior: they sample each arm periodically until they are essentially convinced that it is inferior.

Posterior probability drops below \(1/T\). \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) Essentially convinced that the arm is inferior.

Use a specified tie-breaking rule if the posterior permits tied best arms; the probabilities then sum to one. The \(1/T\) comparison is an intuition, not a stopping rule of Thompson sampling.

In Thompson sampling the selected action is random, with adaptive randomization probabilities depending on past data. This connects naturally with causal inference.

Thompson sampling chooses a random action using past-data-dependent probabilities. \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) Combine with causal inference.

Tuning the prior \(\Pi_0\) can be more reasonable than tuning the UCB interval \(\widehat U_{kt}\).

Inference in adaptive experiments

Analyze data from UCB or Thompson sampling and provide confidence statements about the underlying problem parameters. This is much harder than in the IID setting.

Two natural estimators of \(\mu_k\) are the sample average, \[\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AVG}}=\widehat\mu_{kT} =\frac1{n_{kT}}\sum_{t=1}^T\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}Y_t,\tag{14.11}\] and, under Thompson sampling, the IPW estimator,

Here \(e_{tk}=P(W_t=k\mid\mathcal H_{t-1})>0\) is computed before the current outcome, and equals the earlier \(e_{k,t-1}\). These IPW/AW formulas require randomized exploration; deterministic UCB need not provide positive probabilities for every arm.

\[\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{IPW}}=\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}Y_t}{e_{tk}}.\tag{14.12}\] Because the data collection is adaptive, these estimators need not have asymptotically normal limiting distributions, making confidence intervals difficult.

An adaptively weighted estimator can admit a Gaussian pivot: A Gaussian pivot approaches, or converges in distribution to, \(N(0,1)\). \[\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AW}} =\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}Y_t}{\sqrt{e_{tk}}}} {\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}}{\sqrt{e_{tk}}}}.\tag{14.13}\] For example, suppose current potential outcomes are IID and independent of the past, \(0<b_k^2<\infty\), \(E|Y_t(k)-\mu_k|^4<\infty\), and predictable probabilities satisfy \(e_{tk}\ge ct^{-\alpha}\) for \(c>0\) and \(0\le\alpha<1\). Then \[\widehat V_k^{-1/2}(\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AW}}-\mu_k) \ \Rightarrow\ \mathcal N(0,1),\] where \[\widehat V_k= \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^T \left\{\frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}(Y_t-\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AW}})} {\sqrt{e_{tk}}}\right\}^{\!2}} {\displaystyle\left\{\sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}}{\sqrt{e_{tk}}}\right\}^{\!2}}.\tag{14.14}\] My explanation: a CLT can be recovered because the estimator stabilizes variance. Its variance becomes controllable, allowing a central limit theorem.

Derivation: the martingale behind the estimator.

A martingale \(M_t\) is adapted to the available history \(\mathcal H_t\), satisfies \(E|M_t|<\infty\), and obeys \(E[M_t\mid\mathcal H_{t-1}]=M_{t-1}\). In particular, \(M_t\) uses only information available through time \(t\).

From (14.13), \[\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AW}}-\mu_k =\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}(Y_t-\mu_k)}{\sqrt{e_{tk}}}} {\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^T \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}}{\sqrt{e_{tk}}}}.\] Let the partial sum in the numerator be \[M_t=\sum_{j=1}^t \frac{\mathbf1\{W_j=k\}(Y_j-\mu_k)}{\sqrt{e_{jk}}}.\] \(W_t\) is randomized using the information available before time \(t\) and is independent of \(Y_t(k)\) given the past. Therefore \(M_t\) is a martingale:

\[\mathbb E[M_{t+1}\mid M_1,\ldots,M_t]=M_t,\qquad \text{for all }t.\]

By the weighting scheme, \[\operatorname{Var}[M_t\mid M_1,\ldots,M_{t-1}]=b_k^2, \qquad b_k^2=\operatorname{Var}[Y_t(k)].\tag{14.18}\] Conditioning on the previous partial sum makes this the conditional variance of the new increment as well.

A martingale CLT gives, provided \(e_{tk}\) does not decay too quickly, \[\frac{M_T}{\sqrt{Tb_k^2}}\Rightarrow\mathcal N(0,1).\] adaptively weighted CLT; finite second moments; the sampling probability \(e_{tk}\) should decay more slowly than \(1/t\). This excludes the most aggressive strongly efficient exploration regimes that pull an arm only on the order of \(\log T\) times.

Supplementary conditions for the martingale pivot

One transparent sufficient regime is \(0<b_k^2<\infty\), \(E|Y_t(k)-\mu_k|^4<\infty\), predictable \(e_{tk}\ge c t^{-\alpha}\) with \(c>0\), \(0\le\alpha<1\), and current potential outcomes independent of past data. For \(D_t=\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}(Y_t-\mu_k)/\sqrt{e_{tk}}\), \[E[D_t\mid\mathcal H_{t-1}]=0,\quad E[D_t^2\mid\mathcal H_{t-1}]=b_k^2,\quad T^{-2}\sum_{t\le T}E[D_t^4]=O(T^{\alpha-1})\to0.\] This gives a martingale Lyapunov condition and concentration of \(T^{-1}\sum D_t^2\). The AW denominator has predictable mean scale at least \(T^{1-\alpha/2}\) and fluctuations \(O_P(\sqrt T)\), giving consistency and permitting fitted residuals in the variance. These conditions are sufficient, not necessary. See Hadad et al. (2021) for broader conditions.

Next, \[\frac1{Tb_k^2}\sum_{t=1}^T \left\{\frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=k\}(Y_t-\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AW}})} {\sqrt{e_{tk}}}\right\}^{\!2} \xrightarrow{P}1.\] This is a concentration step; again require the probabilities not to decay too fast. The denominators in \(\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AW}}-\mu_k\) and \(\widehat V_k^{1/2}\) cancel, yielding the Gaussian pivot above.

The denominators of \(\widehat\mu_k^{\mathrm{AW}}-\mu_k\) and \(\widehat V_k^{1/2}\) cancel. \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) The Gaussian pivot in (14.14).
