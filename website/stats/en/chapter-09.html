Lecture 9: Instrumental variables regression

Instrumental variables regression addresses endogenous treatment effects, where \(Y_i(0)\) and \(W_i\) are dependent.

A structural model

Start with a constant-effect model: \[Y_i(w)=Y_i(0)+w\tau,\qquad Y_i=Y_i(W_i).\tag{9.1}\] Unconfoundedness ordinarily means \(\{Y_i(w)\}\perp W_i\); in (9.1), this is \(Y_i(0)\perp W_i\). Rewrite the model as

Constant-effect potential outcomes and unconfoundedness \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) \(Y_i=\alpha+W_i\tau+\varepsilon_i\).

\[Y_i=\alpha+W_i\tau+\varepsilon_i,\tag{9.2}\] where \(\alpha=\mathbb E[Y_i(0)]\) and \(\varepsilon_i=Y_i(0)-\mathbb E[Y_i(0)]\). Under unconfoundedness, \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i\mid W_i]=0\), so OLS of \(Y_i\) on \(W_i\) estimates \(\tau\).

Equation (9.2) still holds with confounding, but the conditional mean of \(\varepsilon_i\) may depend on \(W_i\). OLS then targets \[\begin{aligned} \tau_{\mathrm{OLS}} &=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,W_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(W_i)}\\ &=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\tau W_i+\varepsilon_i,W_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(W_i)} =\tau+\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i,W_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(W_i)} . \end{aligned}\] This ratio is the OLS coefficient. The coefficient differs from \(\tau\) when \(\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon,W)\ne0\); dependence alone need not create linear bias. IV identification requires a nonzero first-stage covariance.

Identification with an instrument

Introduce \(Z_i\): \[Y_i=\alpha+W_i\tau+\varepsilon_i,\qquad\varepsilon_i\perp Z_i, \qquad W_i=Z_i\gamma+\eta_i.\tag{9.4}\] Since \(Z_i\) is uncorrelated with \(\varepsilon_i\), \[\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,Z_i) =\operatorname{Cov}(\tau W_i+\varepsilon_i,Z_i) =\tau\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,Z_i).\] Hence \[\tau=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,Z_i)}{\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,Z_i)}, \qquad \widehat\tau=\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Y_i,Z_i)} {\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(W_i,Z_i)}.\]

My checklist for an IV.

  1. Exogenous: \(\varepsilon_i\perp Z_i\).

  2. Relevant: \(\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,Z_i)\ne0\).

  3. Exclusion restriction: every effect of \(Z_i\) on \(Y_i\) must pass through \(W_i\).

Optimal instruments

Allow a high-dimensional instrument \(Z_i\in\mathcal Z\): \[Y_i=\tau W_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad\varepsilon_i\perp Z_i, \qquad Y_i,W_i\in\mathbb R.\] For a function \(w:\mathcal Z\to\mathbb R\), \[\tau=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,w(Z_i))} {\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,w(Z_i))},\tag{9.8}\] with nonzero denominator. Which transformation \(w(\cdot)\) is optimal? The estimator is \[\begin{aligned} \widehat\tau_w &=\frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Y_i,w(Z_i))} {\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(W_i,w(Z_i))}\\ &=\frac{n^{-1}\sum_i(Y_i-\overline Y)(w(Z_i)-\overline{w(Z)})} {n^{-1}\sum_i(W_i-\overline W)(w(Z_i)-\overline{w(Z)})}, \end{aligned} \tag{9.9}\] where \(\overline Y=n^{-1}\sum_iY_i\), with analogous definitions for the other averages. It solves the estimating equation \[\frac1n\sum_i(w(Z_i)-\overline{w(Z)}) \{Y_i-\overline Y-\widehat\tau_w(W_i-\overline W)\}=0.\tag{9.10}\] The limit is \[\sqrt n(\widehat\tau_w-\tau)\Rightarrow\mathcal N(0,V_w), \qquad V_w=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i)\operatorname{Var}(w(Z_i))} {\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,w(Z_i))^2}.\tag{9.11}\] This limit uses IID observations, finite required moments, centered errors and nonzero limiting first-stage covariance. Its first-order term is\[\sqrt n(\widehat\tau_w-\tau)=\frac{n^{-1/2}\sum_i(w(Z_i)-E[w(Z)])\varepsilon_i}{\operatorname{Cov}(W,w(Z))}+o_p(1).\]

Because \(Z_i\perp\varepsilon_i\) and \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i]=0\), \[\operatorname{Var}\{\varepsilon_i(w(Z_i)-\mathbb E[w(Z_i)])\} =\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i)\operatorname{Var}(w(Z_i)).\] To minimize asymptotic variance, maximize the reciprocal ratio:

Use the reciprocal of the variance-dependent ratio \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The maximization defining \(w^*(\cdot)\) below.

\[w^*(\cdot)\in\operatorname*{arg\,max}_{w'} \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,w'(Z_i))^2}{\operatorname{Var}(w'(Z_i))}.\] A solution is \[w^*(z)\propto\mathbb E[W_i\mid Z_i=z].\]

\(w^*(z)\propto E[W_i\mid Z_i=z]\) \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The best prediction of \(W_i\) from \(Z_i\).

Writing \(m(Z)=E[W\mid Z]\), Cauchy–Schwarz gives \(\operatorname{Cov}(W,w)^2=\operatorname{Cov}(m,w)^2\le\operatorname{Var}(m)\operatorname{Var}(w)\). Equality holds for \(w=a+bm\), \(b\ne0\), if \(\operatorname{Var}(m)>0\). This optimality uses the independence setup above.

Conditioning on covariates.

The condition can be extended to \(\varepsilon_i\perp Z_i\mid X_i\), giving \[\tau(x)=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,w(Z_i)\mid X_i=x)} {\operatorname{Cov}(W_i,w(Z_i)\mid X_i=x)}.\] This motivates a doubly robust estimator of the average effect \(\tau=\mathbb E[\tau(X)]\), and a random-forest estimator of \(\tau(\cdot)\).

This equals \(\tau(x)\) under \(Y=a(X)+W\tau(X)+\varepsilon\), \(E[\varepsilon\mid Z,X]=0\) and a nonzero conditional first stage. With unrestricted individual heterogeneity, a valid instrument need not identify the CATE; suitable monotonicity can instead identify a conditional local effect.

A useful reminder: for independent \(X_1,X_2\), \[\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(X_1X_2) ={}&\operatorname{Var}(X_1)\operatorname{Var}(X_2)\\ &+\mathbb E[X_2]^2\operatorname{Var}(X_1) +\mathbb E[X_1]^2\operatorname{Var}(X_2). \end{aligned}\]

Starred supplement: estimating equations and the delta method.

If functions \(\psi_i\) satisfy \(\mathbb E[\psi_i(\theta)]=0\), and \(\widehat\theta\) is estimated by \[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\psi_i(\widehat\theta)=0,\] then, under differentiability, nonsingular derivative and moment conditions, \[\sqrt n(\widehat\theta-\theta)\Rightarrow\mathcal N(0,V), \qquad V=\frac{\operatorname{Var}(\psi_i(\theta))} {\mathbb E[\psi_i'(\theta)]^2}.\]

Cross-fitting and feasible estimation

The optimal instrument is \(w^*(z)=\mathbb E[W_i\mid Z_i=z]\). The general procedure is:

  1. Fit a nonparametric first-stage regression to estimate \(\mathbb E[W_i\mid Z_i=z]\), obtaining \(\widehat w(\cdot)\).

  2. Use \(\widehat w(\cdot)\) as the instrument in (9.9), obtaining \(\widehat\tau_w\).

However, with many instruments this can create overfitting bias. If \(\widehat w(Z_i)\) is fit using the entire data set, it need not be independent of \(\varepsilon_i\): the fitted instrument can inherit the dependence of \(W_i\) on \(\varepsilon_i\).

My overfitting example.

Let \(W_i=\alpha+\beta Z_i+\eta_i\), with fitted value \(\widehat W_i=\widehat\alpha+\widehat\beta Z_i\). The structural equation is \(Y_i=\gamma+\delta W_i+\varepsilon_i\); substituting the fitted treatment gives \[Y_i=\gamma+\delta\widehat W_i+\{\varepsilon_i+\delta(W_i-\widehat W_i)\}.\]

Rewrite using fitted treatment \(\widehat W_i\) \(\textcolor{blue}{\longrightarrow}\) Return to \(Y_i=\gamma+\delta W_i+\varepsilon_i\): the residual changes too.

My concern is that an overfit first stage may learn the noise in \(W_i\) that is related to \(\varepsilon_i\), without removing this dependence.

If \(Z\) is pure noise, relevance fails. Cross-fitting avoids reusing an observations own noise but cannot create a relevant instrument.

Use \(K\)-fold cross-fitting instead. With \(k(i)\) the fold containing observation \(i\), \[\widehat\tau= \frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(Y_i,\widehat w^{(-k(i))}(Z_i))} {\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(W_i,\widehat w^{(-k(i))}(Z_i))}.\tag{9.13}\] The corresponding fitted instrument is independent of the observation’s error under the stated sampling setup. In particular, if \(\widehat w^{(-k(i))}(z)\) is mean-square consistent for \(\mathbb E[W_i\mid Z_i=z]\), then (9.13) is asymptotically equivalent to the oracle version of (9.9). Write this consistency as

\[\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb E[(\widehat w_n-\mathbb E[W\mid Z])^2]=0,\]

where \(n\) is the sample size. With a nonvanishing first stage, suitable moments and first-stage mean-square consistency, the cross-fitted estimator has the oracle limit. Independence is conditional on each training fold; overlapping training samples do not make all fitted observations mutually independent.

Nonparametric instrumental variables regression

The previous model is linear in treatment \(W_i\): \(Y_i=W_i\tau+\varepsilon_i\).

Generalize it to \[Y_i=g(W_i)+\varepsilon_i,\qquad Z_i\perp\varepsilon_i, \qquad Y_i,W_i\in\mathbb R,\quad Z_i\in\mathcal Z,\] where \(g\) is a general smooth function.

My concern: we cannot simply run a nonparametric regression of \(Y_i\) on \(W_i\), because \[\mathbb E[Y_i\mid W_i=w]=g(w)+\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i\mid W_i=w],\] and the conditional error mean need not vanish.

Since \(Z_i\perp\varepsilon_i\) and \(\mathbb E[\varepsilon_i]=0\), \[\begin{aligned} \mathbb E[Y_i\mid Z_i=z] &=\mathbb E[g(W_i)+\varepsilon_i\mid Z_i=z]\\ &=\mathbb E[g(W_i)\mid Z_i=z] =\int_{\mathbb R}g(w)f(w\mid z)\,dw. \end{aligned} \tag{9.15}\] Here \(f(w\mid z)\) is the conditional density of \(W_i\) given \(Z_i=z\).

A learning procedure:

  1. Estimate \(\widehat f(w\mid z)\) using cross-fitting.

  2. Choose a suitable function class \(\mathcal G\) and minimize the loss to estimate \(g\): \[\widehat g\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{g\in\mathcal G} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left\{ Y_i-\int_{\mathbb R}g(w)\widehat f^{(-k(i))}(w\mid Z_i)\,dw \right\}^{\!2}.\tag{9.16}\]

To solve this in practice, approximate \(g\) with a basis expansion, \[g_K(w)=\sum_{k=1}^K\beta_k\psi_k(w),\] where the \(\psi_k\) are predetermined basis functions. As \(K\) grows, \(g_K\) better approximates \(g\). Then \[\widehat\beta\in\operatorname*{arg\,min}_\beta \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \{Y_i-\widehat m^{(-k(i))}(Z_i)^{\mathsf T}\beta\}^2,\tag{9.17}\] with components \[\widehat m_k^{(-k(i))}(Z_i)= \int_{\mathbb R}\psi_k(w)\widehat f^{(-k(i))}(w\mid Z_i)\,dw.\] My understanding: consistent estimation of \(g(\cdot)\) needs conditions. The integral equation (9.15) is complicated; the basis expansion (9.17) turns it into regression, with a cost in regularization. This connects to the cross-fitted first-stage idea in (9.13).

Identification and regularization

Let \((Th)(z)=E[h(W)\mid Z=z]\). Identification requires injectivity on the chosen class: \(Th=0\) implies \(h(W)=0\) almost surely (completeness). Inversion may still be ill posed, so sieve dimension or other regularization must control instability and approximation error.
