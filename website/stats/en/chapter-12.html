Lecture 12: Evaluating dynamic policies

This topic connects with reinforcement learning. We evaluate a dynamic policy: a policy that changes over time.

There are \(n\) IID individuals \(i=1,\ldots,n\) and times \(t=1,\ldots,T\). At each time we observe time-varying covariates \(X_{it}\) and a treatment \(W_{it}\in\{0,1\}\). At the final time \(T\), we additionally observe an outcome \(Y_i\in\mathbb R\).

To reflect the dynamic structure, every time-varying observation may depend on all earlier treatments. For each \(X_{it}\in\mathcal X_t\), define \(2^{t-1}\) potential values \(X_{it}(w_{1:t-1})\), with \[X_{it}=X_{it}(W_{i,1:t-1}).\] The final outcome has \(2^T\) potential outcomes \(Y_i(w_{1:T})\), and \[Y_i=Y_i(W_{i,1:T}).\] Similarly, write \(W_{it}=W_{it}(W_{i,1:t-1})\): treatment at time \(t\) depends on observed covariate history \(X_{i,1:t}\) and past treatment values.

My intuition.

Picture one individual observed in successive columns \(1,\ldots,T\), with covariates \(X_{i1},X_{i2},\ldots\) and decisions \(W_{i1},W_{i2},\ldots\). Make one decision at each time. The decision at time \(t\) depends on what happened through \(t-1\), and there are \(2^T\) possible final outcomes.

\(X_{i1},W_{i1};\ X_{i2},W_{i2};\ \ldots;\ X_{iT},W_{iT}\) \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) \(2^T\) potential final outcomes \(Y_i(w_{1:T})\)

There are two types of estimand:

  1. Evaluate a fixed treatment sequence \(w\in\{0,1\}^T\): \[V(w)=\mathbb E[Y_i(w)].\]

  2. Evaluate a policy with decision rules \(\pi_t\) mapping the available history to \(\{0,1\}\): \[\begin{aligned} V(\pi)=\mathbb E\bigl[Y_i\bigl(&\pi_1(X_{i1}),\\ &\pi_2(X_{i1},\pi_1(X_{i1}),X_{i2}(\pi_1(X_{i1}))),\ldots\bigr)\bigr]. \end{aligned} \tag{12.2}\]

The second decision depends on covariate history and previous treatment values. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The arguments of \(\pi_2\) in (12.2).

Both problems use potential treatment sequences to define the estimand, including interventions on the assignment distribution that generated the data.

Two natural unconfoundedness-type assumptions are considered:

  1. Sequential unconfoundedness / sequential ignorability: \[\{\text{potential outcomes after time }t\}\perp W_{it} \mid\{\text{history at time }t\}.\] At each time, treatment is unconfounded given the data collected up to that time.

  2. Complete randomization: \[\{\text{all potential outcomes}\}\perp W_{i,1:T}.\] This is not reasonable for the adaptive design considered here.

Identification also requires consistency and positive probabilities of the target actions at every history reachable under \(\pi\). Complete randomization of whole sequences is another possible design, but generally does not describe adaptive assignment.

Treatment–confounder feedback

An example of what can go wrong. All individuals have \(X_{i1}=0\), and \[\mathbb P(W_{i1}=1)=0.5,\qquad \mathbb P(W_{i2}=1\mid X_{i2}=1)=0.8,\qquad \mathbb P(W_{i2}=1\mid X_{i2}=0)=0.4.\] The target compares always treating with never treating: \(\tau=\mathbb E[Y(1,1)-Y(0,0)]\). Analysis shows \[\mathbb E[Y_i\mid W_{i1}=0]=\mathbb E[Y_i\mid W_{i1}=1],\] \[\mathbb E[Y_i\mid W_{i2}=0,W_{i1}=w_1,X_{i2}=x] =\mathbb E[Y_i\mid W_{i2}=1,W_{i1}=w_1,X_{i2}=x]\] for every \(w_1,x\).

The first mistake is to ignore adaptive sampling and use the ordinary difference in means, \[\widehat\tau=\widehat{\mathbb E}[Y\mid W_1=W_2=1] -\widehat{\mathbb E}[Y\mid W_1=W_2=0].\] The second mistake is to stratify on \(X_{i2}\): \[\begin{aligned} \widehat\tau_0&=\mathbb E[Y\mid W_1=W_2=1,X_{i2}=0] -\mathbb E[Y\mid W_1=W_2=0,X_{i2}=0],\\ \widehat\tau_1&=\mathbb E[Y\mid W_1=W_2=1,X_{i2}=1] -\mathbb E[Y\mid W_1=W_2=0,X_{i2}=1],\\ \widehat\tau&=\frac{n_0\widehat\tau_0+n_1\widehat\tau_1}{n_0+n_1}. \end{aligned}\] Why might one try this? We know a statement of the form \(Y_i(\cdots)\perp W_{i2}\mid X_{i2}\). But the stratification would actually need \[Y_i(\cdots)\perp(W_{i1},W_{i2})\mid X_{i2}.\] Consider three types:

Type \(X_{i2}\) if \(W_{i1}=0\) \(X_{i2}\) if \(W_{i1}=1\)
Stable (1) 0 0
Responder (2) 1 0
Acute (3) 1 1

By consistency, the group with \(W_1=1,X_{i2}=0\) contains types (1) and (2), whereas \(W_1=0,X_{i2}=0\) contains only type (1). The group with \(W_1=1,X_{i2}=1\) contains type (3), whereas \(W_1=0,X_{i2}=1\) contains types (2) and (3). Thus, even in a sequentially randomized experiment, this stratification does not control confounding.

Sequential inference under sequential ignorability

Since simple stratification does not work, focus directly on estimating \(V(\pi)\). Define the filtration \[\mathcal F_t=\sigma(X_1,W_1,X_2,W_2,\ldots,W_{t-1},X_t),\] which contains all information available before selecting treatment at time \(t\). Let \(\mathbb E_\pi\) denote expectation when treatment is assigned according to \(\pi\). Then (12.2) becomes \(V(\pi)=\mathbb E_\pi[Y]\).

Define the value function \[V_{\pi,t}(X_1,W_1,\ldots,W_{t-1},X_t) =\mathbb E_\pi[Y\mid\mathcal F_t].\] It measures the expected return from starting in the state represented by \(\mathcal F_t\) and following \(\pi\) thereafter. The overall policy value is \(V_{\pi,0}\).

By the tower property, \[\begin{aligned} &\mathbb E_\pi[V_{\pi,t+1}(X_1,W_1,\ldots,W_t,X_{t+1})\mid\mathcal F_t]\\ &\quad=\mathbb E_\pi[\mathbb E_\pi[Y\mid\mathcal F_{t+1}]\mid\mathcal F_t]\\ &\quad=\mathbb E_\pi[Y\mid\mathcal F_t] =V_{\pi,t}(X_1,W_1,\ldots,W_{t-1},X_t). \end{aligned} \tag{12.6}\]

My interpretation: given \(\mathcal F_t\), the expected future return remains the same when we express it through time \(t+1\). This connects with the Bellman equation, \[V(s)=R(s)+\gamma\sum_{s'}P(s'\mid s,a)V(s').\]

This is a Bellman analogy: for a fixed deterministic policy take \(a=\pi(s)\). The history-based tower identity itself needs no Markov assumption or discount factor.

If we have a good estimate of \(V_{\pi,t+1}\), seek a good estimate of \(V_{\pi,t}\) and continue backward until we recover \(V(\pi)\).

Write \(X_{T+1}=Y\). The joint distribution factors as \[P_\pi(X_1,W_1,\ldots,X_T,W_T,Y) =P_\pi(X_1)\prod_{t=1}^T P_\pi(W_t\mid\mathcal F_t) P_\pi(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,W_t).\] Unconfoundedness means that the non-treatment factors do not depend on the policy: \[P_\pi(X_1)=P(X_1),\qquad P_\pi(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,W_t) =P(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,W_t),\quad\text{for all }\pi.\]

The effect of treatment on the subsequent covariates does not depend on the selecting policy. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The invariant transition factor \(P(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,W_t)\).

Inverse-propensity weighting

To make (12.6) useful with off-policy data, change measure: the actual data were not generated by \(\pi(\cdot)\). \[\begin{aligned} V_{\pi,t} &=\mathbb E_\pi[V_{\pi,t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t]\\ &=\mathbb E\left[\left. \frac{P_\pi(W_t,X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t)} {P(W_t,X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t)}V_{\pi,t+1} \right|\mathcal F_t\right]\\ &=\mathbb E\left[\left. \frac{P_\pi(W_t\mid\mathcal F_t)P_\pi(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,W_t)} {P(W_t\mid\mathcal F_t)P(X_{t+1}\mid\mathcal F_t,W_t)} V_{\pi,t+1}\right|\mathcal F_t\right]\\ &=\mathbb E\left[\left. \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=\pi_t(\ldots,X_t)\}} {P[W_t=\pi_t(\ldots,X_t)\mid\mathcal F_t]} V_{\pi,t+1}\right|\mathcal F_t\right]. \end{aligned}\]

Change from the observed policy to \(\pi\); cancel the transition factors. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The two transition factors cancel.

Apply the change of measure at every \(t=1,\ldots,T\). Since \[V(\pi)=\mathbb E[V_{\pi,1}(X_1)],\] \[V_{\pi,1}(X_1)=\mathbb E\left[\left. \frac{\mathbf1\{W_1=\pi_1(X_1)\}} {P[W_1=\pi_1(X_1)\mid\mathcal F_1]} V_{\pi,2}(X_1,W_1,X_2)\right|\mathcal F_1\right],\]

repeated substitution eventually leaves \(V_{\pi,T+1}=\mathbb E_\pi[Y\mid\mathcal F_{T+1}]=Y\) and gives

\[V(\pi)=\mathbb E\left[ \left\{\prod_{t=1}^T \frac{\mathbf1\{W_t=\pi_t(\ldots,X_t)\}} {P[W_t=\pi_t(\ldots,X_t)\mid\mathcal F_t]}\right\}Y\right].\]

Should this condition on \(\mathcal F_1\)? \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) The matching probability \(P[W_1=\pi_1(X_1)\mid\mathcal F_1]\).

Yes: the denominator must condition on \(\mathcal F_1\). Independence of \(W_1\) and \(X_1\) alone does not make the matching probability constant when \(\pi_1(X_1)\) varies.

An IPW estimator is \[\widehat V_{\mathrm{IPW}}(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\gamma_{iT}(\pi)Y_i, \qquad \gamma_{it}(\pi)=\gamma_{i,t-1}(\pi) \frac{\mathbf1\{W_{it}=\pi_t(\ldots,X_{it})\}} {P[W_{it}=\pi_t(\ldots,X_{it})\mid\mathcal F_{it}]}.\] The recursion starts at \(\gamma_{i0}(\pi)=1\).

My interpretation: average outcomes matching \(\pi\), and use IPW to remove the confounding effects of observed time-varying variables. If the weights, that is, each conditional assignment probability, are known, \(\widehat V_{\mathrm{IPW}}\) is unbiased.

Backward regression adjustment

Use (12.6) and sequential unconfoundedness, iterating backward. At \(t=T\), \[\begin{aligned} V_{\pi,T}(X_1,W_1,\ldots,X_T) &=\mathbb E_\pi[Y\mid\mathcal F_T]\\ &=\mathbb E_\pi[Y\mid\mathcal F_T,W_T=\pi_T(X_1,W_1,\ldots,X_T)]\\ &=\mathbb E[Y\mid\mathcal F_T,W_T=\pi_T(X_1,W_1,\ldots,X_T)]. \end{aligned}\] Learn \(\widehat V_{\pi,T}\) directly by nonparametric regression. Given a reasonable estimate of the next value function, \[V_{\pi,t}(X_1,W_1,\ldots,X_t) \approx\mathbb E[\widehat V_{\pi,t+1}(\ldots,X_{t+1}) \mid\mathcal F_t,W_t=\pi_t(\ldots,X_t)].\tag{12.12}\] Continue backward until an estimate of \(V(\pi)\) is recovered.

Unlike IPW, quantify how regression errors propagate through backward iteration. \(\textcolor{blue}{\longrightarrow}\) \(Q\)-learning

The displayed recursion evaluates a fixed policy. Optimal \(Q\)-learning additionally maximizes over actions; the error-propagation issue arises in both.

A doubly robust estimator

We now have both IPW and regression. Equation (12.12) yields a value estimate \(\widehat V_{\pi,1}(X_1)\), and hence \[\widehat V_{\mathrm{REG}}(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\widehat V_{\pi,1}(X_{i1}).\]

Backward regression (12.12) \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) \(\widehat V_{\pi,1}\), then \(n^{-1}\sum_i\widehat V_{\pi,1}(X_{i1})\)

If we trust \(\widehat V_{\pi,2}\) more than \(\widehat V_{\pi,1}\), use it to debias the earlier estimate: \[\widehat V(\pi)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left[ \widehat V_{\pi,1}(X_{i1})+ \gamma_{i1}(\pi)\{ \widehat V_{\pi,2}(X_{i1},W_{i1},X_{i2}) -\widehat V_{\pi,1}(X_{i1})\}\right].\]

Weight \(\gamma_{i1}\); correction term. \(\textcolor{red}{\longrightarrow}\) \(\gamma_{i1}(\widehat V_{\pi,2}-\widehat V_{\pi,1})\)

\[\widehat V_{\mathrm{AIPW}}(\pi) =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\left[ \gamma_{iT}(\pi)Y_i+ \sum_{t=1}^T\{\gamma_{i,t-1}(\pi)-\gamma_{it}(\pi)\} \widehat V_{\pi,t}(X_{i1},\ldots,X_{it})\right].\] This generalizes AIPW from the nondynamic case.

Continue the correction until \(V_{\pi,T+1}=Y\). \(\textcolor{black}{\longrightarrow}\) The full sequential AIPW estimator above.

What conditions make the sequential AIPW estimator unbiased?

Supplementary derivation: sequential double robustness

Let \(q_t\) be fixed or independently trained value functions, \(q_{T+1}=Y\), and \(\gamma_0=1\). The summand equals \[q_1+\sum_{t=1}^T\gamma_t(q_{t+1}-q_t).\] If every treatment probability is correct, weighted expectations telescope to \(E_\pi[Y]\) for arbitrary \(q_t\); unbiased regressions are unnecessary. If every \(q_t=V_{\pi,t}\), each residual has conditional mean zero under the target action, allowing positive predictable propensity models. Marginally unbiased fitted regressions alone do not suffice, especially when the same observations are reused. Estimated nuisances generally require consistency/product-rate conditions for asymptotic claims. See Jiang and Li (2016).
