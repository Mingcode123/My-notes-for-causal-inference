Chapter 7. Beyond controlling for confounders

This chapter concerns the effects of controlling for given variables. In \(X\to Y\leftarrow T\), controlling \(T\) when studying \(X\to Y\) can make the effect of \(X\) clearer and reduce the variance of its estimate. A second sketch has \(B\to A\to X\to Y\) and \(B\to Y\): \(B\) is related to both \(X\) and \(Y\); after adjusting for \(B\), \(A\) is an additional predictor of \(X\). Adding \(A\) can reduce the variation left in \(X\) and increase the variance of its coefficient estimate. \[\hat\sigma^2=\frac{1}{n-2}\sum_i(y_i-\hat y_i)^2,\qquad \widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\beta_2)=\frac{\hat\sigma^2}{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}.\] For homoskedastic simple OLS, \(\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\beta_2)=\hat\sigma/\sqrt{\sum_i(x_i-\bar x)^2}\). Larger sample variation gives a smaller standard error. In multiple regression the relevant variation is the residual variation after the other controls, with the corresponding residual degrees of freedom.

Confounding is the bias from failing to control for a common cause. Controlling a common effect can induce selection bias; controlling a mediator blocks part of the total effect.

Bad controls and participation.

For a treatment that affects whether the outcome is zero, an RCT makes the mean difference causal. For \(Y\ge0\), decompose the difference into a participation component and a conditional-positive-outcome component: \[\begin{aligned} E[Y\mid T=1]-E[Y\mid T=0] ={}&\{P(Y>0\mid T=1)-P(Y>0\mid T=0)\}E[Y\mid Y>0,T=1]\\ &+\{E[Y\mid Y>0,T=1]-E[Y\mid Y>0,T=0]\}P(Y>0\mid T=0). \end{aligned}\] The first part is the effect on the probability of a positive outcome, and distinguishes customers’ participation from spending ability. Restricting analysis to participants gives

\[\begin{aligned} E[Y_{i1}\mid Y_{i1}>0]-E[Y_{i0}\mid Y_{i0}>0] ={}&\underbrace{E[Y_{i1}-Y_{i0}\mid Y_{i1}>0]}_{\text{causal effect among participants}}\\ &+\underbrace{E[Y_{i0}\mid Y_{i1}>0]-E[Y_{i0}\mid Y_{i0}>0]}_{\text{selection bias}}. \end{aligned}\] The last term compares the untreated potential outcome among those who would participate under treatment with that among those who would participate without treatment. The algebraic components above do not separately become causal effects merely because treatment is randomized.
