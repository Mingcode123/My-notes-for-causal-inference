Chapter 5. The usefulness of linear regression

For individual \(i\), suppose \(Y_{1i}=Y_{0i}+k\): the treatment effect is constant, and the target is \(k\). In a simple regression, \[Y_i=\beta_0+kT_i+u_i,\qquad \beta_1=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,T_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(T_i)}.\] With random assignment, the difference between the two group means, and hence the population regression coefficient on binary \(T\), is the ATE.

Binary treatment: the two fitted values differ by \(k\), illustrated here with \(k<0\).

Multiple regression accounts for the factors related to \(T_i\): \[y_i=\beta_0+kT_i+\underbrace{\beta_1X_{1i}+\cdots+\beta_KX_{Ki}}_{\textcolor{blue}{\text{auxiliary covariates}}}+u_i.\]

My understanding: use \(X\) to predict \(T\), then take the residual \(\textcolor{blue}{\longrightarrow}\) \(\widetilde T=(I-P_X)T\): the part of \(T\) that \(X\) cannot explain; \(X\) includes the intercept

The residualized treatment supplies the variation used for its regression coefficient: \[k=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(Y_i,\widetilde T_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\widetilde T_i)}.\] Here the covariance expression describes the population linear projection; its sample counterpart is \(\hat k=\widetilde T'Y/(\widetilde T'\widetilde T)\).

What happens if a multiple regression is needed, but I omit a variable and use a simple regression? Consider the constant-effect linear model \[\mathrm{Wage}_i=\alpha+k\,\mathrm{Educ}_i+\underbrace{A_i'\beta}_{\textcolor{blue}{\text{ability term}}}+u_i.\] Under \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathrm{Educ},u)=0\), omitting ability gives \[\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(\mathrm{Wage}_i,\mathrm{Educ}_i)}{\operatorname{Var}(\mathrm{Educ}_i)} =k+\underbrace{\beta'\delta_A}_{\textcolor{red}{\mathrm{OVB}}}.\]

Regress ability \(A\) on education \(\textcolor{blue}{\longrightarrow}\) \(\delta_A\): the coefficient vector of that projection

The extra term is omitted variable bias. In this correctly specified linear model, including the confounders removes this omitted-variable term.

An RCT breaks the relationship between treatment and confounding factors; regression holds those factors fixed when making the comparison.

The causal interpretation here uses the stated constant-effect model and exogeneity. With heterogeneous effects or an inadequate adjustment model, the adjusted regression coefficient need not equal the unweighted ATE.
